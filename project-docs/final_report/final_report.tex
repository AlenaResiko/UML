\begin{abstract}
		\textit{How much embedding dimensionality is needed for a given corpus? Can a model
				trained on a simpler domain transfer effectively to semantically richer tasks? How does sensitivity to
		embedding dimensionality vary across corpora?}

		To answer these questions, we study how domain and embedding dimensionality interact in
		unsupervised sentence encoders. Using two extreme corpora, \textbf{Taylor Swift lyrics
		and Nakul Verma's papers}, we train \textbf{contrastive BERT models from 4D to 767D}
		and evaluate geometry and transfer learning properties.

		Surprisingly, lower-dimensional \textbf{models trained on lyrics (4-256D) outperform
		paper-trained models on STS-B}, with papers only catching up at 767D. This suggests
		that simple, high-frequency text can be an efficient pretraining source for compact
		encoders, while complex text needs far more dimensions to expose useful structure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sentence embeddings are mappings from high-dimensional linguistic signals to lower-dimensional structures
whose geometry captures the semantic meanings of sentences. Latent space embeddings are crucial in NLP tasks.
Recent progress—including BERT \cite{devlin2019bert} for token embeddings and then Sentence-BERT
\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} for sentence embeddings—has significantly improved sentence embedding quality.
In practice, resulting embeddings are typically used as is (in 768D or 1024D for BERT-based models) without
any consideration given to decreasing the compute or memory on downstream tasks via a lower embedding
dimensionality. However, the nature of the data itself and the downstream task might require a different
dimensionality, which could make the resulting embeddings not only more efficient but also useful.

We train sentence representation models using a two-step procedure and evaluate their ability to generalize
across domains. Our evaluation steps include 1) assessing transfer learning of the models trained on one
particular dataset (either pop songs or scientific papers) on general, out-of-distribution tasks, and 2) the
resulting geometry of in-domain and out-of-domain data embedded with our trained models. Through this
analysis, we can also infer the intrinsic dimensionality of the topic-specific data used to train the models
under evaluation.

On a high level, we take a step towards answering the following questions: Which conceptual domains
(represented by the data used to train our models) allow for smaller-dimensional representations that still
capture meaning and retain transfer learning abilities? How much can we decrease the embedding dimension
while retaining useful representations?

\section{Related Work}
Widespread adoption of transformer encoders began with BERT \cite{devlin2019bert}, which learns deep
contextual token representations that can be repurposed for sentence-level embeddings. Sentence-BERT
\cite{reimers2019sentencebert} extends BERT with a siamese architecture and pooling scheme, enabling
efficient cosine-similarity retrieval and establishing a strong supervised benchmark for sentence embeddings.
\cite{li2020sentenceembeddingdim} analyze sentence embeddings produced by pretrained language
models, showing strong anisotropy and proposing geometric corrections to improve isotropy and downstream task
performance.

Contrastive learning provides the main algorithmic backbone for modern sentence encoders.
\cite{wang2020contrastive} show that contrastive objectives implicitly trade off two geometric
pressures—alignment of positive pairs and uniformity of embeddings on the hypersphere—offering a framework we
directly use to interpret our models. \cite{wang2021divergence} further study the behavior of
contrastive losses such as InfoNCE under different design choices, explaining how temperature, sampling, and
margin parameters induce distinct embedding geometries. Building on these ideas, SimCSE \cite{gao2021simcse}
introduces a simple but powerful contrastive objective for sentences, where dropout-generated views serve as
positive pairs and hard negatives emerge naturally from in-batch sampling.

Recent work also focuses on the dimensional structure of sentence representations.
\cite{wang2023} study the performance of low-dimensional sentence embeddings from pretrained encoders,
decomposing the impact of the encoder and pooler and showing that substantial dimensionality reduction is
often possible without dramatic loss in quality. Their work served as our main reference for this study.
\cite{amit2021intrinsicdim} investigate how
intrinsic dimensionality governs generalization, arguing that effective models adapt their representation
dimensionality to the complexity of the underlying data manifold—a perspective that motivates our focus on
how different corpora support useful low-dimensional sentence embeddings.
\section{Methodology}
We use contrastive learning as described in SimCSE \cite{gao2021simcse}. SimCSE's training objective pulls
positive pairs together in the embedding space and pushes negative pairs apart. In our context, positive and
negative pairs are defined by closeness in the same document, creating a self-supervised setting. SimCSE was
previously shown to be successful on STS benchmarks and transfer tasks, which is why we use it as the main
method for learning sentence meaning. We train and evaluate two architectures: encoder + pooler (BERT + a
feed-forward NN) and encoder + PCA (BERT + PCA). Below we briefly discuss implementation details. All the
code is available at \href{https://github.com/AlenaResiko/UML}{github.com/AlenaResiko/UML}.

\subsection{Encoder + Pooler}
The encoder maps tokens to latent representations, and the pooler maps hidden states to
low-dimensional embedding of sentences. \cite{wang2023} found that performance loss can be decomposed into
separate encoder and pooler contributions, which is the motivation for using a two-step training procedure.
First, we freeze the parameters of the pooler and fine-tune the encoder, achieving the best version of BERT
for a particular dataset. Then, we freeze the encoder and train
only the pooler. We do each step in three epochs. This procedure allows us to reduce the encoder loss and the
pooler loss sequentially.

We load \texttt{bert-base-uncased} weights from Hugging Face for BERT with the output in 768D. For the
pooler, we construct a
fully connected network with a hidden-size $\times$ hidden-size layer, tanh activation, followed by a
hidden-size $\times$ projection-size
fully-connected layer (following \cite{wang2023}). We apply mean pooling to aggregate token embeddings into
sentence embeddings. The final output dimension is determined by the pooler. For our experiments, we sweep
through $d \in \{4,16,64,256,767\}$.

Note that BERT produces position-aware token representations, so the order information describing tokens'
relative positions in a sentence is not lost.

In training we apply the SimCSE trick: a sentence passed as is and then passed one more time with a dropout
mask applied to the underlying model should have
similar embeddings. For each anchor sentence, we compute the multi-positive normalized temperature-scaled
cross-entropy loss and optimize using the Adam optimizer.

\subsection{Encoder + PCA}
The encoder is again BERT, followed by PCA allowing us to customize the final output dimension. For a given
projection dimensionality $d$, we take the first $d$ principal components. We sweep through $d \in
\{4,16,64,256,767\}$ to match the encoder + pooler experiments.

\subsection{Training domains}
We choose domains to capture different styles, word frequencies, and modes of communication:

1) Taylor Swift lyrics (short, repetitive, low-entropy)

2) Nakul Verma's ML papers (long, information-dense)

Each song and each paper section are treated as one document, and neighboring sentences within a document
form positive pairs. The corpora are token-matched for training compute parity (since BERT operates on the
token level, not sentence level). After pre-processing, we split the text into sentences, which we consider
to be the smallest complete unit. Our pre-processing methodology is discussed below.

\subsection{Data Pre-processing}

For the lyrics dataset, we found a Kaggle repository containing the entire Taylor Swift discography and
downloaded it for our lyrics corpus. The cleanup included removing sequential duplicate sentences, filtering out
song and album metadata, and splitting the lyrics into individual sentences using the NLTK sentence
tokenizer. Each newline was treated as an end-of-sentence marker.

For the scientific dataset, we used the arXiv API to download Nakul Verma's papers in pure LaTeX form. There was
substantial cleaning and processing work involved from that point. First, we removed latex commands,
equations, figures, tables, and references using regex patterns. Next, we split the cleaned text into
sentences. These two actions required a fair amount of user judgement, and diminished the size of the usable corpus.

Given two corpora, we create pairs of similar sentences in a self-supervised manner through assigning
similarity based on sentence distance: if sentences are within a distance 5 of each other, they are deemed similar.

\subsection{Training Objective}
The goal of contrastive learning is to pull together representations of positive examples and push apart
negatives: in this way we learn correct neighborhood structure. We use contrastive training as in
\cite{gao2021simcse}. Let $P_i$ be a set of positive (near neighbors and itself) examples for each sentence
and $e_i$ be embeddings of each sentence. The per-pair similarity score function is a dot product normalized
by temperature $\tau$ :
\[
\exp S_{ij} = \exp\!\left(\frac{e_i^\top e_j}{\tau}\right)
\]
We want to maximize the ratio of similarity of positives to similarity of non-positives, averaged over $M$
pairs in a batch:
\[
\mathcal{L}
=
\frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{M} - \log\!\left(
\frac{\sum_{j \in P_i} \exp(S_{ij})}{\sum_{\substack{j=1 \\ j\neq i}}^{M} \exp(S_{ij})}
\right).
\]
Viewed as a probability, the function inside the $\log$ is a conditional probability $p(j|i)$ of positive
examples $j$ given an anchor sentence $i$. The training objective $\mathcal{L}$ is the cross-entropy over
these conditional probabilities.

\subsection{Evaluations}

\begin{itemize}
\item Geometry:
\begin{itemize}
\item Alignment and uniformity losses (\cite{wang2020contrastive}) on in-domain
and out-of-domain test sets. Alignment measures how close positive pairs are, and
uniformity captures how evenly embeddings are spread on the hypersphere (how much of the provided space they occupy).
\item t-SNE embeddings of sentences from test songs and papers. We aim to see if the two types are separated
into distinct clusters by the models and examine the cluster geometry qualitatively.
\end{itemize}
\item Transfer learning: STS-B Spearman rank correlations of sentence pair embeddings.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{PCA} projections of the frozen BERT encoder at the same target dimensions are
used as a baseline.

\section{Results and Analysis}

\subsection{Spearman Rank on STS-B}
The results on lyrics-trained models rise sharply between 4D and 256D, peaking long
before the full BERT size of 768D (see Fig. \ref{fig:stsb}). Paper-trained models, in
contrast, only come to full potential at 767D, hinting at a much \textbf{higher intrinsic
dimensionality of scientific text}. Since STS-B is out-of-distribution for both training
corpora, the gap between the performance of songs- and papers-trained models cannot be
attributed to lyrics being much "closer" to STS-B than papers. In fact, qualitatively we could argue that
STS-B is closer to scientific text than to pop song lyrics. Instead, the performance gap reflects how
contrastive fine-tuning under a low-dimensional bottleneck \textbf{reallocates capacity
of the model}.

On the lyrics corpus, the contrastive task is relatively simple and repetitive, so the
model can solve it without drastically reshaping the pre-trained BERT geometry. A small
output dimension is enough to compress lyrics-specific structure while preserving generic
semantic directions that still transfer to STS-B.

On the papers corpus, the contrastive task is much harder: the model must separate dense,
technical sentences within and across sections. In low dimensions, the encoder and the pooler
are forced to specialize for fine-grained distinctions, which distorts general-purpose
semantics and hurts STS-B transfer. Only when we increase the projection size up to
256-767D does the paper-trained model regain enough capacity to encode both in-domain
nuance and broad semantic structure, at which point it overtakes the lyrics-trained model.

The PCA baseline stays near zero Spearman rank across all projection sizes, suggesting
that \textbf{linear compression alone cannot expose semantic structure} -- the pooler must learn it.

\subsection{Uniformity and Alignment}
All plots in Fig. \ref{fig:losses} show the \textbf{alignment vs uniformity tradeoff}: higher
dimensions improve uniformity but degrade alignment. For PCA specifically, uniformity
losses are much smaller than alignment losses for all dimensions, indicating that
embedded sentences occupy the full hypersphere but do not preserve the local similarity structure.

For contrastive training, \textbf{in-domain data} (eg. songs embedded with a model
trained on songs) \textbf{becomes well-spread} (good uniformity) but not overly tight,
and \textbf{out-of-domain data} often collapses, achieving \textbf{artificially low
alignment with poor global geometry}. This collapse likely occurs because the model is
forced to project unfamiliar sentence structure into a space shaped only by in-domain
positives, over-compressing local neighborhoods with unseen examples without preserving meaningful global
structure. In addition, papers-trained models behave more similarly in terms of both
losses on different domains, while songs-trained models have larger oscillations when
domains are switched, hinting at more stable geometry learned by papers-based embeddings
across domains.

\subsection{Songs and Papers Clusters Embedded with t-SNE}
Even with only 4 dimensions, the embeddings already separate lyrics from papers as two
distinct clusters (see Fig. \ref{fig:tsne}), indicating that coarse domain identity
survives aggressive compression. However, in 4D the clusters are elongated and less
compact, with visibly distorted shapes. In addition, there is a group of paper sentences that end up being
much closer to the songs cluster (concentrated in the bottom right part of the plot). At 16 dimensions, the
clusters become rounder,
and local neighborhoods look more stable. This suggests that increasing dimensionality
from 4D to 16D does not create domain separation from scratch, but instead improves the
within-domain geometry: it reduces compression artifacts and yields a smoother manifold,
which is consistent with the concurrent improvement in STS-B performance.

\subsection{Ablation on the Training Procedure}
For songs-trained models, with smaller dimensions (4, 16), the two-step training process
yields the best Spearman rank correlations, showing how \textbf{important the pooler is
for extreme dimensionality reduction} (see Fig. \ref{fig:ablation}). However, at certain
higher dimensions training only the encoder yields the best results. Training only the
pooler is not sufficient for learning useful representations for songs-based models.

For papers-based models, we see a similar trend, but the dimension cutoff at which the
pooler stops giving much benefit is higher, somewhere between 128D and 256D (again
hinting at the higher intrinsic dimensionality of scientific data). In addition, only
training the pooler with the frozen BERT backbone yields much better Spearman rank
results for papers-trained models than for songs-trained models, perhaps hinting
that the frozen BERT encoder is initially better suited for academic text.

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Lyrics-trained models dominate at low dimensions (4–256D) on STS-B}, while
papers-trained models only overtake at 767D $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{scientific text appears
inherently higher-dimensional}.
\item \textbf{Uniformity and alignment conflict} with each other, with
\textbf{papers-trained models being more stable} when transitioning from in-distribution
to out-of-distribution data.
\item \textbf{Low-dimensional embeddings} are able to \textbf{separate domains (even at
4D)}, but more meaningful local structure emerges only at 16D or more.
\item Linear compression \textbf{(PCA) is insufficient for transfer learning} tasks in
our experiments: contrastive learning is necessary to expose semantic structure even at
small dimensions. We recommend retraining, rather than linearly transforming embeddings, when the target
dimensionality changes.
\item The \textbf{two-step training} procedure of the encoder + pooler setup is \textbf{necessary} for
satisfactory performance in \textbf{extreme dimensionality reduction} settings. When we increase the output
dimension, training the pooler becomes less important.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Challenges and Limitations}
We note that all reported results depend on a specific choice of backbone (BERT-base),
corpus size, and contrastive neighborhood definition. In addition, while STS-B Spearman
rank results provide an insightful semantic probe, it represents only one notion of
transfer learning performance.

\paragraph*{Defining Similarity} Drawing inspiration from \cite{gao2021simcse}, we
explored enriching our unlabeled dataset with self-supervised positive pairs. We landed on sentence proximity
as a straightforward and effective method to generate these pairs without additional labeling. However, many
other approaches exist: only using the same sentence embedded by the same network with random dropout masks
twice is one interesting example. One could also use a large network to label similarity, allowing for
knowledge distillation. Our approach could deem unrelated sentences that appear consecutively in a document
similar, which is an important limitation introducing a lot of noise.

\paragraph*{Contrastive Loss Fine-tuning} By design, contrastive fine-tuning mainly acts on local geometry:
it pulls positive pairs together and pushes negatives apart, encouraging a more uniform distribution of
representations on the unit hypersphere. Because most of the representational structure is inherited from the
pretrained BERT encoder, the optimization is effectively upper-bounded by these initial representations. Our
procedure does not attempt, nor is it capable of, substantially re-architecting the global geometry of the
embedding space. Thus, while contrastive learning can refine local neighborhoods and yield meaningful
improvements, it is not expected to produce dramatic, paradigm-shifting gains in sentence embedding quality.

\paragraph*{Scope} Due to time and resource constraints, we limited our experiments to two narrow domains of
the English language. A more comprehensive study would include a much broader array of domains, self-labeling
algorithms, and backbone architectures beyond \texttt{BERT-base-uncased} (e.g., RoBERTa, DeBERTa, etc.).

\subsection{Future Directions}
For future work, we would like to explore \textbf{additional domains} (such as news, code
comments, etc.), try other \textbf{embedding models beyond BERT}, use
\textbf{alternative training objectives} (for instance, non-contrastive methods), enrich the suite of
\textbf{evaluation methods}, and implement competing self-supervised \textbf{sentence pair labeling
algorithms}. This wider range of methods can allow for better capture of sentence structure's geometry.

Expanding the scope a bit, it would be interesting to move beyond all-purpose sentence embeddings and explore
\textbf{task-specific embeddings} and \textbf{multimodal embeddings}, modeling language in a
\textbf{hierarchical manner}, moving beyond Euclidean spaces into \textbf{non-Euclidean geometries}, and
exploring the notion of \textbf{intrinsic dimensionality} more rigorously.
